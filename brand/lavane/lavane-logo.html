Logo design · Leaf-Crystal · Lavané

One grain of salt, one leaf. That is the whole idea.

Lavané is a pure vegetarian restaurant that takes its name from lavaṇa, the Sanskrit word for salt. The mark joins the two things the brand stands for: the honest essential ingredient, and a kitchen that never serves meat. The wordmark is set in Roman inscriptional capitals, so it reads as a European dining room anywhere in the world.

The name · Lavané

Lavané: one accent makes it European

Without an accent, English speakers say “LAV-ane”, stressing the first syllable and rhyming it with “vane”. “Lav” is also British slang for a toilet. An accent on the final E moves the stress to the end and shows at a glance that the name is Continental. The same move made café, rosé and purée sound European.

Lavanesaid “LAV-ayn”

Neutral and easy to type, but gives no cue for how to say it. Most English speakers will stress the wrong syllable.

Lavanèsaid “la-va-NEH”

The grave accent on a final E is an Italian pattern, as in caffè and perché. It feels like a trattoria. French almost never ends a word in -è, so French readers may find it odd.

Chosen
Lavanésaid “la-va-NAY”

The acute accent is the accent of European food words: café, rosé, soufflé, sauté. Its “ay” ending is also how the final e in Sanskrit lavaṇe sounds. The accent that makes the name European also gets the Sanskrit pronunciation right.

Pronunciation guide

How to read é and è

Both marks sit on the letter e and change its sound. Look at which way the line leans.

é
Accent aigu · acute · the line rises /
“ay”, closed and short
  • Like Hindi ए in देश or केला
  • Like English “day”, but stop before the “ee” glide at the end
  • Mouth: smile slightly, jaw nearly closed
è
Accent grave · grave · the line falls \
“eh”, open
  • Like Hindi ऐ as most people say है or बैठ
  • Like English “bed” or “get”
  • Mouth: let the jaw drop a little more
Memory trick. The é line goes up and the mouth stays closed and smiling: “ay”. The è line goes down and the jaw drops: “eh”.
No accent, no sound. In French, a plain final e is usually silent, so “Lavane” would be read “la-VAN”. The é is what makes the last syllable heard.
ê sounds like è. The hat-shaped circumflex, as in crêpe or forêt, is also the open “eh”.
Typing é. On a phone, press and hold e. On a Mac, press Option+E, then E. On Windows, hold Alt and type 0233 (0232 gives è).
lavaNÉ
ला · वा · ने almost the same sound as Sanskrit लवणे

Keep all three syllables light and even, with a small lift on the last one. Do not stress the first syllable.

Uses your device's French voice, which varies by device.
Ownable detail

The accent is a grain of salt

In the wordmark, the font's stock accent is replaced with a tiny facet cut from the Leaf-Crystal: half basil, half leaf green. Up close it is a brand detail. From across the room it reads as an ordinary accent.

Using the accent
  • Always write it with the accent in signage, menus, copy and press: Lavané.
  • Drop it where systems can't handle it. Domains, email addresses and social handles stay plain: lavane.com, @lavane.
  • Teach the sound once. Put “la·va·NAY” under the name on the first page of the menu and on the website footer.
Before launch
  • Trademark both spellings, with and without the accent, in each launch market.
  • Say it out loud to a few French and Hindi speakers to check that “la-va-NAY” comes naturally to both.
  • Brief your team. Staff should greet guests with “Welcome to la-va-NAY”. How the team says it is how the city will say it.
Design language

Native at the root, European on the plate

Every logo decision traces back to one of three brand truths. If a shape could not be explained by one of them, it was cut.

From lavaṇa, salt

Faceted, crystalline cuts

Salt is a crystal. The symbol is drawn only with straight facets and clean cut-lines, like a mineral. It signals purity and honesty without saying “organic”.

From pure vegetarian

A leaf, never a garnish

The crystal's silhouette is a leaf, its cut-lines are veins, and it ends in a stem. Vegetarian is the brand's only distinction, so it lives inside the symbol rather than in a badge.

From a global table

Roman capitals, wide tracking

“Lavané” sounds French at first sight. Flared Roman capitals lean into that, so the name sits easily beside a bistro in Paris or a dining room in Pune. Devanagari appears only as a quiet secondary signature.

Concepts

Five routes, one recommendation

Each route tests a different way of telling the same story. All five share the palette and wordmark, so any of them can be developed further.

Top pick

1 · Leaf-Crystal

Symbol + wordmark

A salt crystal cut into the shape of a leaf. The facets are the veins, and the stem anchors it as something grown. The dark left half and lighter right half give it a gem-like turn of light.

Why it wins: it is the only route that says salt and vegetarian in a single shape. It works as a one-colour stamp, an app icon and an embossed seal, and it does not depend on the tagline to be understood.

2 · Salt Grain

Isometric crystal

A single cubic salt grain, shaded on three faces. Very clean and modern.

Trade-off: it says “salt” clearly but not “vegetarian”, and a cube can read as tech or logistics.

3 · Arch & Sprout

Doorway emblem

A Roman arch that rises into a soft Indian point, with a sprout and a saffron sun inside. It reads as a doorway into the restaurant.

Trade-off: the warmest story, but the most illustrative, and it loses detail at small sizes.

4 · V-Sprout

Wordmark only

The V in Lavané is made of two leaves, so the name carries the vegetarian promise by itself.

Trade-off: elegant on signage, but no standalone symbol for icons and stamps. Worth keeping as an alternate wordmark.

5 · Seal

Badge with लवण

A round seal holding the Leaf-Crystal, with लवण (lavaṇa) set in Devanagari. This is where the native root becomes visible.

Trade-off: too detailed for a primary logo. It works best as a secondary mark on packaging, wax seals and the bill folder.

Nature-led routes · New

Same story, drawn by hand

These routes keep the salt-and-leaf idea but trade the crisp, digital facets for curves, stems and textures you would find in a kitchen garden. Each one can sit alongside the Leaf-Crystal or replace it.

Soft Leaf

Recommended evolution

The Leaf-Crystal redrawn with curved edges and curved veins. A single saffron grain of salt sits by the tip. It keeps the two-tone halves, so it is clearly the same brand, just warmer.

Best for: the primary logo if the crystal feels too technical. It pairs with the italic wordmark below for menus and hand-written moments.

Sprig & Salt

Botanical

A basil sprig with three grains of salt falling beside it. It reads like a cook seasoning a dish by hand.

Italic lavané

Script wordmark

A soft lowercase italic with a leaf for the accent. It feels like a handwritten menu and suits napkins, windows and social posts.

Olive Wreath

Emblem

Two olive branches around the name, tied with saffron berries. The most classically European route, suited to engraving and certificates.

River Pebble

Stamp

The Soft Leaf inside a hand-shaped pebble. It works as a stamp on clay, kraft or terracotta, and as a social avatar.

In nature

Lavané on natural materials

Wood, linen, terracotta, wax and canvas, with no screens and no hard plastic.

Next steps

Taking the Leaf-Crystal forward

  1. Confirm the verse. Share the exact Sanskrit line and its source so the Devanagari on the seal and menus is correct.
  2. Refine the symbol. Fine-tune facet angles and the stem curve at 16px, 64px and signage size, and make a heavier version for small print.
  3. Customise the wordmark. Marcellus is the base. A designer should redraw the A crossbars and the N to make it fully ownable before trademark filing.
  4. Check the name. Run trademark and domain searches for “Lavané” and “Lavane” in your launch markets.
